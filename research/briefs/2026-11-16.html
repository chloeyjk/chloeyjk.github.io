Prediction-powered judge rankings, and three different ways "the benchmark" moves under you

4 new entries this week: a martingale-based prediction-powered inference method for ranking LLMs from adaptively collected human feedback plus judge scores, an audit showing a reasoning-failure taxonomy's reported rate depends more on classifier tie-break order than on the model, a decomposition of 1.26 million Chatbot Arena votes showing human judging standards drift upward over time, and a formal account of why offline benchmarks and live A/B tests can rank the same models differently.

New this week

LLM Judges

Prediction-Powered LLM Ranking with Adaptively Collected Human Feedback

Method: Prediction-Powered Ranking (PP-Rank) combines LLM-judge predictions with adaptively collected human feedback, choosing data-driven prediction weights that minimize asymptotic variance and using a joint martingale analysis to keep inference valid despite the adaptive sampling and judge bias.

Why it matters: Evaluation platforms collect human feedback adaptively to focus on informative comparisons, which breaks the i.i.d. assumptions behind standard confidence intervals; this gives narrower intervals and valid simultaneous ranking certification while cutting human-labeling cost.

Validity

Agreeing on the Answer but Not the Reason: Measurement Limits of LLM Reasoning Failure Taxonomies

Method: Defines a reasoning-failure category and measures it across twelve models and eleven languages; finds its reported share swings from 26.4% to 76.4% purely from reordering classifier tie-break tests, and uses Cohen's kappa against native-speaker raters to show agreement on raw answer correctness (kappa=0.932) far exceeds agreement on the failure category (kappa=0.093).

Why it matters: A growing body of work reports failure-taxonomy percentages as if they were stable measurements; this shows the number is largely an artifact of classifier procedure and prompt wording, and proposes four cheap checks (co-occurrence reporting, tie-break permutation, shared validation, disagreement audits) before trusting one.

In Practice

AI Isn't Getting Dumber, We're Getting Pickier: Rising Standards in Human Evaluation of Large Language Models

Method: Analyzes 1.26 million blind Chatbot Arena votes on version-locked model pairs (May 2023–Aug 2024) and estimates month effects shared across models, using them to remove the common drift from each model's own pairs; checks hold within the same voter, the same pair, across arrival cohorts, and with prompt controls.

Why it matters: The share of "both are bad" votes rose steadily for pairs of models that never changed, while relative rankings stayed flat — meaning absolute human-eval scores drift even when nothing about the models does, which matters for leaderboards and for any human feedback used to train models.

Validity

When Both Sides Adapt: The Challenges of Evaluating Interactive LLM Systems

Method: Formalizes offline benchmark evaluation and live A/B testing as estimators of two different estimands — a common-distribution effect versus an induced-distribution effect created by user adaptivity — shows their standard difference-in-means estimators converge to different targets, and derives a sufficient condition for when the two methods' model rankings are guaranteed to agree.

Why it matters: Users change what and how they prompt based on what a model gives them, so an offline benchmark and a live experiment can rank the same two models differently even when both are estimated correctly; a stylized linear model also shows user adaptivity can inflate estimation variance beyond the usual sampling noise.

Considered, not added

  • Do Verifier Judgments Survive Abstraction? Measuring Verification Transport for LLM Agent Evaluation (arXiv 2611.18471): a careful controlled-view study of verifier reliability across abstraction boundaries (31% transport failure under macro abstraction), but the contribution is the experimental design and measured failure rates rather than a statistical estimator or inference method.
  • Black-Box Uncertainty Quantification for Large Language Models via Ensemble-of-Ensembles (arXiv 2611.18453): rigorous statistics (law-of-total-covariance decomposition, a bias-corrected nested Monte Carlo estimator), but it quantifies a single model's own aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty in its answers, not the evaluation of models against each other or a benchmark.
  • When Evaluators of AI Agents Disagree: An Empirical Study on tau2-bench (arXiv 2611.14581): reports judge-vs-native-scoring agreement at 56.2%, but the paper doesn't name or report an inter-annotator agreement statistic for its own two-annotator check, so the statistical contribution is thin.
  • Comparative Analysis of Agent Evaluation Frameworks: Stability, Detection, and Discovery in Enterprise Analytics Agents (arXiv 2611.14493): compares three evaluation harnesses with F1 and precision-recall, but no inferential statistics beyond repeated-measurement stability thresholds.
